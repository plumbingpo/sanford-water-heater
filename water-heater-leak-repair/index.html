Water around a water heater is one of the most common calls we get, and the first question is always the same: where is it actually coming from? Water runs downhill and pools at the lowest point, so the puddle is often nowhere near the source. It might be the temperature and pressure relief valve or its discharge line, the drain valve near the bottom of the tank, the hot and cold connections on top, condensation on a cold tank, or a nearby pipe that has nothing to do with the heater. It might also be the tank itself, and that answer changes everything. In Sanford, FL, we start every leak call by finding the true source.

Our diagnosis is methodical. We dry the area, look at the T&P valve and the end of its discharge pipe, inspect the drain valve, and follow the hot and cold lines up to the fittings on top of the tank. On gas units, we look under the burner access for water, which can point to condensation or, more seriously, a failing tank. If the relief valve has been discharging, we measure static water pressure and check for thermal expansion, since a closed plumbing system without an expansion tank can spike pressure every time the burner runs. After all of that do we recommend a fix.

The repair depends on the diagnosis. Valve and fitting leaks are the good news: relief valves, drain valves, dielectric nipples, and flex connectors ride on our trucks, and we can often add an expansion tank or pressure-reducing valve the same day if pressure caused the problem. A leak from the tank body is the bad news, because a rusted-through tank is past saving, and we will say so rather than sell a patch. In both cases, we help limit future damage with a properly drained pan, a leak alarm or automatic shutoff, and a quick lesson on your shutoff valve. Warning Signs to Watch For

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • A puddle or damp spot on the floor around the base of the tank
  • Water inside the burner compartment of a gas heater
  • Mineral buildup or rust around the hot and cold connections
  • Water dripping from the relief valve or out of the end of its discharge pipe
  • A water bill that rises without any change in usage
  • A musty odor or soft floor around the heater location
  • A weeping drain spigot on the lower front of the heater
  • Rust-tinted hot water and signs of seepage near the base

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Most leak mistakes happen before any tool comes out. Water travels along pipes and the outer jacket before it drips, so the puddle rarely marks the source, and harmless condensation on a gas unit or a cold tank can pass for a leak. Guessing leads to replacing the wrong valve or, worse, missing a tank that is on its way out. Safety matters here too: a relief valve that is discharging is protecting the tank, and plugging it is dangerous. Old fittings can snap when forced, and water around a 240-volt electric heater is a real shock hazard.

We work through leaks step by step and fix the cause along with the drip. We dry the unit, let new water reveal the source, test water pressure when the relief valve is involved, and check for expansion issues that cause weeping valves. We replace valves and fittings with quality parts, swap plastic drain valves for brass, and add an expansion tank or pressure-reducing valve when pressure is the root problem. If the tank has failed, we tell you directly and explain replacement options, including the drain pan and leak protection that can keep the next failure from soaking your floors.

How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Follow these tips from our technicians:

  • Replace a depleted anode rod before the steel tank becomes the next target for corrosion
  • Have household pressure measured, since high pressure wears out relief valves and fittings faster
  • Annual flushing removes sediment that traps heat against the tank floor
  • A battery leak alarm beside the heater costs little and can catch a problem days sooner
  • Place the heater in a drain pan with a line to a floor drain or outdoors, especially above finished space
  • Glance at the heater monthly for damp spots, rust, or crust on the fittings
  • Make sure a closed plumbing system has a working expansion tank to absorb heated water
