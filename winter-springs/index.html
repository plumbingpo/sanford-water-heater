Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Winter Springs

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on descaling every year if their water is hard.

Take a look at the age of your water heater. The rating plate on the tank usually encodes the manufacture date, and its age says a lot about what comes next. Most tank models reach 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and regularly descaled tankless units around 20. If your heater is past that range and is also showing rusty water, rumbling, or moisture at the base, it's a strong candidate for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and less stressful than a rushed emergency install once the tank fails.

The sooner you call about a hot water problem, the smaller the repair tends to be. A sediment layer that's just starting to rumble can often be fixed with a flush, yet that same buildup, neglected through a few more years, can create hot spots on the tank floor and burn out the lower element on an electric model, turning a simple visit into a replacement. Save our number in your contacts today so help is one call away as soon as something sounds or looks wrong. A quick call saves time, money, and cold showers.

Common Water Heater Problems in Winter Springs

The majority of water heater failures we see in Winter Springs start with the water itself. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be a real factor: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why annual descaling is worth keeping up with in hard-water areas.

Then there's age. When the sacrificial anode rod is gone, often well before the tank itself looks old, the steel begins corroding from within, and a slow leak at the base often shows up next. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from failed thermocouples and draft problems to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Well water may bring sediment, iron, or sulfur into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water often means a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: a thermal expansion tank where a check valve or pressure regulator creates a closed system, a properly routed T&P discharge line, a drain pan where a leak would hit finished floors, and earthquake straps where local code requires them. All of these can be fixed, and most cost far less when caught early.
